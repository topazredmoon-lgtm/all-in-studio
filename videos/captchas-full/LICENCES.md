## Why the risk is low, and what is left
- **Licences checked at the source.** Wikimania 2015 and "Introducing reCAPTCHA v3": licence on the Commons file page (the
  v3 video was published by Google Search Central under CC BY on YouTube, then imported to Commons). CCC talks: the mp4
  files carry `copyright=Licensed to the public under https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/` (checked with ffprobe
  on all three, 2026-10-09).
- **Content ID.** None of these uploaders is a news network. A claim would need someone to have put the footage into
  Content ID; the CC licence then makes a dispute straightforward (cite the licence and the attribution block).
- **Most residual risk (still low): the four Google v3 clips (g10, g11a, g11, g13).** Google owns the footage and could in
  theory have Content ID on its own channel. CC BY cannot be revoked for copies already made, so a claim would be released
  on dispute. Estimate: claim ~3 %, revenue lost for good <1 %.
- **Share-alike.** The Wikimania clips are CC BY-SA 3.0: cutting them into the film may make the film an adaptation that
  must carry CC BY-SA. This allows ads and selling; it only means others may reuse the film with credit. It costs no revenue.
- **People on screen.** The 36C3 clip shows two click workers' photos from the researchers' own talk; the 35C3 clip shows
  the speaker's screencast of a reCAPTCHA grid. Both are part of the CC BY recordings; we name the talk on screen.
- **Text citations, not footage.** Some facts still cite Vox or The New York Times as small text plates (c02r, f01b). That
  is a citation, not their footage or audio, and carries no claim risk.
- Whole film: at least one claim ≈ 5 %; this video's revenue lost to a claimant ≈ 1 %; any strike < 1 %; channel
  demonetization (reused content) < 5 % (original narration, clips ≈ 30 % of runtime, each set up by the narrator).
