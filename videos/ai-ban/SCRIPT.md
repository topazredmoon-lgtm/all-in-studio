# THE COMING AI BAN · rewrite plan (2026-10-11)

Base: the owner's `owner_script.txt` (spine kept: the ban, the deepfake bank panic, open weights, chips, Big Tech's
licence, the stolen commons, the two-tier future). Owner gave full licence to change it.

## What changes and why
1. **True beats loud.** The owner's script states predictions as facts ("within 36 months... outlawed", "a classified
   warning", "chips with kill-switches", "every prompt tied to your ID"). On a monetised channel that is a strike
   risk, and viewers who check it stop trusting us. The real story is scarier: *every piece needed for a ban already
   exists or has been tried.* So the spine becomes: **"Nobody has banned AI. But look at the pieces on the table."**
   Facts are said as facts (sourced in `SOURCES.md`); predictions are said as "if".
2. **Takeaways.** Each act lands one thing the viewer didn't know: encryption was legally a weapon until 1996; the
   US already put AI "brains" under export control once (Jan 2025); a few hundred dollars removes an open model's
   safety training; the man who runs ChatGPT asked the Senate for an AI licence; and how ordinary people won the last
   time (a book, a court case: "code is speech").
3. **Friendly jargon (owner flag ~3:47 = the old "black-market proof" scene).** Every hard word gets one plain
   sentence and a picture: *weights* (the AI's brain, a file of numbers you can download), *open vs closed*,
   *fine-tuning* (a short extra lesson), *jailbreak*, *export control*, *licence regime*, *regulatory capture*.
4. **Narrator has a side.** Neutral until the facts are laid out (act 5), then: "I'll be honest: this is the part I
   hate." Ends with hope and a job for the viewer, not doom.
5. **Ending flip.** The owner's "it will be called sedition" becomes the encryption callback: they called strong locks
   weapons once; now they're on every phone, because people fought. Same fight, and now you know the pieces.

## Narration v1 (2026-10-11) · source row in brackets · [CLIP] = real clip slot (PD / CC only)

### 1 · Cold open (the 1-min sample)
- Open the AI app on your phone. Ask it anything. It answers, and it never asks who you are.
- Now picture the day it does. Your licence number, or no answer.
- [CLIP c01 · Altman, US Senate, 16 May 2023: "If this technology goes wrong, it can go quite wrong."] [1b]
- That's Sam Altman, the man who runs ChatGPT, in front of the US Senate. And then he asked for something strange. [1a]
- [CLIP c02 · Altman: "I would form a new agency that licenses any effort above a certain scale of capabilities..."] [1a]
- A licence to build AI. Like a driving licence, but for building a mind. It sounds safe. But we've seen this before.
- Until nineteen ninety-six, America listed strong encryption, the maths that keeps your messages private, as a munition. On the same list as tanks and missiles. [3a]
- Nobody has banned AI. Not yet. But almost every piece you'd need to do it has already been built, or tried.
- By the end of this video, you'll know each piece, who wins if they snap together, and how ordinary people beat this once before.

### 2 · The lock that broke
- First, why would anyone want to lock AI away? Follow the money.
- Banks run on one rule: make sure it's really you. A signature. An ID. Your face. Your voice.
- In early twenty twenty-four, a finance worker in Hong Kong joined a video call with his company's finance chief and several colleagues. [2a]
- He knew their faces. He knew their voices. Every one of them was a deepfake: a fake video made by AI, playing live. [2a]
- He made fifteen transfers. About twenty-five million dollars, gone. [2a]
- [CLIP · CC talk on real-time deepfakes]
- Last year, Altman himself warned bankers at the US Federal Reserve. Some banks still let your voice unlock your account. In his words, AI has fully defeated that. [10b]
- So here's the first piece. When nobody can prove they're human, someone will demand that everyone prove who they are.

### 3 · Two easy words (the friendly jargon act; the owner's ~3:47)
- To see why that scares governments, you need two words. Don't worry, they're easy.
- Word one: weights. An AI's brain is a giant file of numbers. Billions of them. Those numbers are the weights.
- Some companies keep that file locked on their own computers. You can talk to it, but you can never take it home. That's a closed model.
- Others publish the file. You download it, and it runs on your own laptop, even offline. That's an open model.
- Word two: fine-tuning. It's a short extra lesson that changes how the AI behaves.
- Companies use it to teach manners. The AI learns to say no to dangerous requests.
- But a lesson can be unlearned. In twenty twenty-three, researchers stripped those manners out of Meta's Llama 2 for less than two hundred dollars. [9a]
- A later team did it to a newer model in about five minutes, for under a dollar. [9a]
- [CLIP · CC talk on jailbreaking chatbots]
- And once a file is out, it's out forever. The internet has no recall button.
- Criminals noticed. In twenty twenty-three, a hacker forum sold a chatbot called WormGPT, built to say yes to anything, for sixty euros a month. [9d]
- Others don't even bother. They steal the keys companies use for paid AI, and run their scams on someone else's bill. [9c]
- That's piece two. A safety lock that can be picked for pocket change.

### 4 · The pieces on the table
- Now look at what's already been tried. Piece three: treat the brain like a weapon.
- In January twenty twenty-five, the US government did it. It put the weights of the most powerful closed AI models under export control. [4a]
- Export control means you need permission to send something abroad. The same kind of rules that cover military technology.
- Open models were left out, and the rule was scrapped four months later. But the template now exists. [4a]
- [CLIP · US government or CC talk]
- Piece four: the chips. A bill in Congress, the Chip Security Act, would make export AI chips report where they are. [7a]
- Nvidia, which makes most of them, answered in public: no back doors, no kill switches, no spyware. [7b]
- Piece five: your name. Since twenty twenty-three, China has made deepfake apps verify every user's real identity. With a phone number, or an ID card. [13f]
- And in Europe, the AI Act is already law. It doesn't ban chatbots. But it built the machinery: rules, audits and fines for whole kinds of AI. [5a] [5c]
- None of these pieces is a ban. Not on its own.

### 5 · Who wins (the narrator takes a side)
- So ask the question nobody on that stage wanted to answer. If the pieces snap together, who wins?
- Remember Altman's licence. Licences cost money. Lawyers, audits, paperwork, every single year.
- A company worth hundreds of billions can pay that. A student with a laptop can't. Neither can a ten-person startup.
- There's a name for this: regulatory capture. It's when the biggest players help write the rules, and the rules keep everyone else out.
- Not my words. In twenty twenty-three, Meta's own chief AI scientist, Yann LeCun, accused the heads of the top AI labs of trying exactly that. [11b]
- [CLIP · CC talk on regulation / big tech]
- I'll be honest with you. This is the part I hate.
- Fraud is real, and people get hurt. Rules can help. But a licence only giants can afford isn't safety. It's a gate.
- And we'd be locked out of something we helped build.

### 6 · Built from us
- Because these brains were trained on us. Our books, our articles, our forum posts, our code.
- Last year, Anthropic agreed to pay one and a half billion dollars to authors, over pirated books it trained on. [12b]
- A judge said training itself can be fair use. Pirating the books was not. [12b]
- The New York Times is still in court against OpenAI. Who owns what's inside these brains is a fight that's wide open. [12a]
- [CLIP · CC talk on AI and copyright]

### 7 · Two internets
- So picture the end state, if every piece clicks together.
- The banks, the governments and the biggest companies keep the raw, powerful models.
- Everyone else gets a filtered assistant. Rented by the month, logged, and tied to your name.
- It wouldn't arrive with a speech on TV. It would arrive in small print. A terms-of-service update. An ID check. A chip rule.
- And here's the twist. The criminals wouldn't even notice. They already have the files.
- A ban like that doesn't stop the crooks. It stops you.

### 8 · The last time
- So can ordinary people stop this? They already did, once.
- Back to the nineties. A programmer named Phil Zimmermann gave the world PGP: free, strong encryption for anyone. [3b]
- The US government opened a criminal investigation. Sharing that code abroad could count as exporting a weapon. [3b]
- So his side did something brilliant. MIT Press printed the entire source code as a book. Try banning a book. [3d]
- In nineteen ninety-six, a federal judge ruled in a separate case: source code is speech. [3e]
- That same year, the case against Zimmermann was dropped, and encryption came off the munitions list. [3a] [3b] [3d]
- Today it protects every message on your phone. Because people pushed back.
- [CLIP · CC talk, crypto wars / digital rights]
- That's the real takeaway. Rules like this aren't written in one night. They're written in public, and the public can push back.
- So learn what open weights are. Run a model on your own computer, once. Read the bills before they pass.
- Look at the AI on your phone again. It still answers without asking who you are. Whether it stays that way isn't up to them. It's up to us.
