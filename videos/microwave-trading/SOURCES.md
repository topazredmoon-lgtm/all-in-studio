## 1. Physics & Optical Index of Refraction
- **Silica Glass Refractive Index ($n \approx 1.4682$)**: Light in single-mode silica fiber ($c/n$) propagates at approximately $204,190\text{ km/s}$—roughly $68.1\%$ of the speed of light in vacuum ($c \approx 299,792\text{ km/s}$), representing an unavoidable $\approx 31.9\%$ latency penalty.
  - Source: Agrawal, Govind P. *Fiber-Optic Communication Systems*. 4th ed., Wiley, 2010.
- **Atmospheric Refraction ($n \approx 1.0003$)**: Microwave and millimeter-wave radio frequencies propagate through the troposphere at $99.97\%$ the speed of light in vacuum, giving wireless links a permanent $\approx 46\%$ speed advantage over fiber optic cables covering the same geographical distance.
  - Source: ITU-R Recommendation P.453-14, "The radio refractive index: its formula and refractivity data."

## 2. Spread Networks & The Allegheny Fiber Trench (2010)
- **The $300 Million Trench**: Spread Networks spent over $300M blasting an unswerving straight line through the Allegheny mountain range to lay 825 miles of dark fiber between Chicago (CME in Aurora, IL) and New Jersey (Nasdaq in Carteret, NJ).
- **Latency Gain**: Reduced round-trip latency from $\approx 16\text{ ms}$ (over standard curved rights-of-way along railways/turnpikes) to $13.1\text{ ms}$ (one-way $\approx 6.55\text{ ms}$). Firms paid up to $300,000/month or $14M upfront leases for 5-year access.
  - Source: Lewis, Michael (2014). *Flash Boys: A Wall Street Revolt*. W. W. Norton & Company.
  - Source: Steiner, Christopher (2012). *Automate This: How Algorithms Came to Rule Our World*. Portfolio/Penguin.

## 3. The Microwave Revolution & Tower Acquisitions (2011–2014)
- **The Microwave Leap**: In 2011–2012, networks built by McKay Brothers, Tradeworx, and Jump Trading replaced fiber with chains of 14–18 line-of-sight microwave towers between Chicago and New Jersey.
- **Latency Collapse**: Round-trip latency plummeted from $13.1\text{ ms}$ to $8.2\text{ ms}$—rendering Spread Networks' $300M investment virtually obsolete for latency-critical trading within 18 months.
- **Federal Authorizations**: FCC Form 601 Part 101 Private Operational Fixed Point-to-Point microwave filings documented the exact tower locations across Indiana, Ohio, and Pennsylvania.
  - Source: Laumonier, Alexandre (2014). "The Microwave Network between Chicago and New York." *Sniper in Mahwah*.
  - Source: US Federal Communications Commission (FCC) Universal Licensing System (ULS), Call Signs: WQNE589, WQOQ802, WQPF468.

## 4. The European Arms Race & The Belgian Cabbage Field (2013–2016)
- **London-Frankfurt Corridor**: Trading between London (Slough LD4) and Frankfurt (Equinix FR2) triggered a tower war across the English Channel. McKay Brothers acquired access to the decommissioned Cold War NATO microwave mast in Swingate, Dover.
- **The Houtem Mast Conflict**: In Houtem, Belgium, Vigilant Global and New Line Networks fought over adjacent farmland parcels. When an 800-foot mast was planned, competitors attempted to build an even taller mast in an adjacent cabbage field to block the optical line-of-sight and preserve their 3-nanosecond advantage.
  - Source: Financial Times (2016). "High-frequency traders clash over mast on Belgian border."
  - Source: Wall Street Journal (2016). "The 800-Foot Tower Fueling Wall Street's Speed War."

## 5. Modern Frontiers: Hollow-Core Fiber & Free-Space Lasers (2020–Present)
- **Hollow-Core Fiber (HCF)**: Photonic bandgap and nested anti-resonant nodeless fibers (NANFs) guide light inside an air/vacuum capillary core rather than solid glass, restoring propagation velocity to $>99.7\% c$ and eliminating the 31% glass speed penalty.
  - Source: Poletti, F. et al. (2013). "Towards high-capacity fibre-optic communications at the speed of light in vacuum." *Nature Photonics*, 7, 279–284.
- **Atmospheric Lasers & Hardware Trimming**: Optical laser transceivers (free-space optics) and physical cable jacket stripping at datacenter server cages to shave picoseconds.
  - Source: Budish, Eric, Peter Cramton, and John Shim (2015). "The High-Frequency Trading Arms Race: Frequent Batch Auctions as a Market Design Response." *Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 130(4), 1547–1621.
